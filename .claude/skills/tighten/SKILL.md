# Tighten: a wording pass aimed by measurement

A wording pass that fixes what reads badly usually saves **nothing** in pages. One aimed at
what costs a line saves pages *and* improves the prose. This skill is the second kind.

It is the aiming half only. `linkage prose stats` owns the other half — which patterns are
overused relative to the author's own corpus, and where they are (`--instances FAMILY`).
Run that first when the question is voice; run this when the question is length.

## The idea

Prose sets in whole lines, so shortening a paragraph pays nothing until its character count
crosses a multiple of the line width. What matters is not how long a paragraph is but how
far past the last boundary it sits.

A paragraph 7 characters past a boundary loses a whole typeset line for 7 characters of
editing. One 70 past needs 70. Sorting paragraphs by that number turns a diffuse effort into
a ranked list of cheap wins — the same move as sorting by value/weight in a knapsack.

Read the other way, the same number says where text is **free**: a paragraph needing `cut`
characters to lose a line has `room = width - cut` characters before it gains one. That is
the number to use when inserting a new sentence rather than cutting one.

The boundary case matters: a paragraph whose length is an *exact* multiple of the width is
the worst case in both directions, not the best. It fills its last line exactly, so
shortening costs a full line and one added character gains one. `scripts/overhang.py` handles
this; a naive `chars mod width` reports 0 there and sorts those paragraphs to the top of the
list, where they waste the most effort.

## Procedure

1. **Calibrate the width** (below). Everything depends on it.
2. **Rank.** `python3 scripts/overhang.py report paper/*.tex --width N`
   Sorted cheapest-first: `cut`, `room`, effective characters, occupied lines, opening words.
3. **Work top-down.** Each paragraph is now a well-posed task: *remove at least `cut`
   characters without losing anything a reader needs.* That is checkable, which is what turns
   taste into work — "make this better" has no stopping rule, so an editor either does nothing
   or rewrites everything.
4. **Stop the moment a paragraph crosses.** The next line costs a full width; continuing is
   wasted effort at best and over-compression at worst.
5. **Skip large `cut` values** unless the paragraph has obvious slack. Past roughly half the
   width the economics are poor; spend the effort on the next cheap paragraph.
6. **Re-run and let the build be the oracle.** Predictions are good to about ±15% per
   paragraph, ±0.3 page on a running total.

### What the edits actually are

A hard character target makes you attack the cheapest words first, and in a first draft the
cheapest words are systematically the ones carrying no information. This is why the pass
improves the prose rather than merely compressing it: the cost function and the quality
function are accidentally aligned, because slack in prose *is* the low-information material.

Much of what surfaces is already named by `linkage prose`'s families — use its tag vocabulary
rather than inventing one. `SIGNPOST`, `SALIENCE` and `BOOSTER` are throat-clearing;
`ADVERB` the anthropomorphic process adverbs; `NEGPAR` the negative parallelism, where saying
what the thing *is* usually suffices; `PARALADDER` a restatement that a tight paragraph does
not need twice.

Three kinds fall out of the ranking that no regex catches, and they carry most of the saving:

- **Throat-clearing that is not a fixed phrase.** *"The content of (5) is that the whole
  family is generated by"* to *"So the whole family is generated by"*. *"The construction
  needs one hypothesis"* to *"One hypothesis is needed"*.
- **Nouns restating their verbs.** *"must itself be an admissible measurement"* to *"must
  itself be admissible"*. *"a delay of longer duration"* to *"a longer delay"*.
- **Sentences written twice in one paragraph.** Common in a first draft, and the largest
  single saving.

**Do not pattern-match and delete.** Every family above is fine in small doses and it is the
overuse that hurts — which is exactly why the rate against the author's own corpus, not the
raw count, is what `prose stats` reports. Some instances are compensation for an imprecise
sentence opening; there, fixing the opening is the stable fix and the tic disappears with it.

## Calibrating the width

**From a built PDF** — preferred, one command, no modelling:

    python3 scripts/overhang.py calibrate dist/main.pdf

In a justified column every full line has the same width in points but a different number of
characters, so the counts form a hump with a long left tail of paragraph ends, headings and
display math. The tool takes the **mode**, which is robust where the maximum (one stray wide
line) and the mean (dragged down by the tail) are not. Needs `pdftotext` (poppler-utils).

**Without a PDF**, fit the width against known section start pages: the cumulative estimated
cost before each section must land in `[start - 1, start)`. Sweep the width and keep the
values satisfying every interval. This needs a fuller cost model than `report` implements
(displays, theorem environments, lists, headings), so prefer the PDF route.

Both routes were run on the same LNCS document and agreed on **79**. That number is LNCS body
text; do not carry it to another template.

## Guardrails

- **Never edit a theorem-like environment.** In an article-kit repository these are ledgered,
  and `linkage check` compares `% shared with blueprint` statements byte-for-byte against the
  nodes they name. `overhang.py` refuses to list them, so an editor working from the report
  cannot be tempted. Same for displays, lists, figures and verbatim.
- **No claim changes, no hypothesis weakens.** The character target must never be met by
  dropping a condition.
- Structural moves dominate micro-editing. Merging two short paragraphs recovers the ragged
  last line of one; deleting a paragraph recovers all of it; a display typically costs three
  to four prose lines. If you are over by a page, look at displays before prose.

## The failure mode — always follow with a correctness pass

Dropping a noun that looks redundant can silently change the scope of a predicate.

> *"is a realizable measurement"* became *"is realizable"*. The first asserts membership in a
> class; the second is a bare adjective a reader attaches to whatever is nearest. The result
> was grammatically clean and mathematically false, and survived until an adversarial review
> round caught it.

**A wording pass on technical prose must be followed by a correctness pass, and not by the
same reader.** Expect roughly one introduced error per 75 edits.

Neither of the framework's guards will catch it. A statement ledger hashes whether a statement
*changed*, not whether it is *correct*, and this class of error lives in the prose *around* the
statements, where no ledger, no `\lean` tag and no `linkage check` looks. The
`fidelity-review` skill audits statement-to-blueprint correspondence, not the surrounding
exposition. Budget a reading pass, or a blind adversarial round, after any tighten pass that
touched more than a handful of paragraphs.

## What does not work, measured

- **Sub-line edits.** A pass of ten tic fixes, aimed at what read badly, measured **exactly
  zero** pages. This is the whole reason the skill exists.
- **Float geometry.** Shrinking a figure and cutting its caption also measured exactly zero.
- Neither moves a multi-sheet document unless it happens to cross a page break. Only removing
  whole typeset lines moves a page count.

## Where it does not apply

The method needs a fixed line width, reflowed text, and a countable notion of effective
characters. LaTeX, a fixed-column word-processor template, any two-column proceedings format
— fine. HTML and anything responsive has no line width, and the mechanism disappears.

## Provenance

Developed on the `hcs` SSVM submission, September 2026. Seventy-five edits across eight files
took the predicted prose from 278 to 241 typeset lines, about 0.8 of a page, and the build
from over the limit to 11.96 of 12. The reserve list drawn up for the same overrun went
unspent.
